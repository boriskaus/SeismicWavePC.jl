Models

The model

A model is a GMG CartData (coordinates in km, z up; constant spacing in x and y, see below for z) with the fields

  • rho [kg/m³] (or g/cm³ with density_unit = :g_cm3, as in OpenSWPC),
  • vp, vs [km/s],
  • optionally Qp, Qs (without them the model is elastic). Q is constant between fq_min and fq_max, and the velocities apply at fq_ref (keywords of simulate; defaults 0.05, 5 and 1 Hz),
  • optionally epsilon, delta, gamma: VTI anisotropy (see below).

Cells with vp = vs = 0 are air: the free surface and topography follow from them. Cells with vs = 0 and vp > 0 are water (oceans, lakes). A CartData of size (nx, 1, nz) is a 2D model (P-SV by default, wave = :sh for SH waves). Any GMG tool can be used to build the model: layers, volcanoes, spheres, slabs, geographic data and projections.

Topography is a staircase of cells. As in OpenSWPC, the shear modulus at a shear-stress point is the harmonic mean of the four cells around it, so it is zero next to air, also at the inner corner at the foot of every step. A top cell one cell wide in x (or y) then has no shear coupling to the ground in that direction and does not move in it. With simulate(...; free_surface = :corners) these inner corners (one cell of the four with μ = 0) take the harmonic mean of the three solid cells; flat surfaces and outer corners are unchanged. In 2D tests on stair-stepped slopes of 10–30° against the exact flat surface, it makes the surface seismograms 2–4 times more accurate (misfit 17 instead of 34–52 % at 20 cells per S wavelength, 3–4 instead of 6–12 % at 80). See Validation for a benchmark with tilted planes and a hill.

Topography on a curvilinear grid

The staircase of cells is accurate away from the surface, but close to a sloping surface its errors reach 10–30 % at 16 points per S wavelength (see Validation). A 2D or 3D model can instead be a deformed CartData: a terrain-following mesh whose top row of nodes is the topography. It is then simulated on a curvilinear grid with summation-by-parts (SBP) finite differences: the free surface lies exactly on the topography, and the scheme is provably stable (the discrete energy is conserved, or dissipated at the absorbing boundaries). The SBP operators are the boundary-optimized dual-pairing operators of Mattsson, Niemelä & Winters (2027, J. Comput. Phys. 568, 115399; order 7), from SummationByPartsOperators.jl (Ranocha, 2021, J. Open Source Softw. 6(64), 3454). The method and its relation to published SBP-SAT schemes for seismic waves: Curvilinear solver: method and related work.

topo(x) = 3.0 * exp(-x^2 / 50)                                   # [km]
moho(x) = -7.0 + 1.5 * sin(2π * x / 40)
mesh = curvilinear_mesh(range(-72, 52, length=249), [topo, moho]; dz=0.5, bottom=-80)
X, Z = mesh.x.val, mesh.z.val
Phase = ifelse.(Z .< moho.(X), 2, 1)                              # layers
add_ellipsoid!(Phase, zeros(size(Phase)), mesh; cen=(0, 0, -3), axes=(3, 3, 1.5), phase=ConstantPhase(3))   # a magma chamber
vp_, vs_, rho_ = (5.5, 6.5, 3.8), (3.2, 3.75, 2.0), (2600.0, 2900.0, 2400.0)
model = addfield(mesh, (; rho=getindex.(Ref(rho_), Phase), vp=getindex.(Ref(vp_), Phase), vs=getindex.(Ref(vs_), Phase)))
result = simulate(model; tmax=20.0, sources, stations)           # dt from the stability limit
  • The mesh. curvilinear_mesh makes the mesh from surfaces (functions or surface CartData): a grid line follows the topography and every interface with a large jump in the material (e.g. a Moho). Columns are vertical. Fill the material at the nodes with the usual GMG tools. GMG ≥ 0.10.2 makes the same mesh, with all its model tools working on it (add_layer!, add_box!, add_ellipsoid!, interpolation of data onto it): curvilinear_grid(sbp_nodes(-72, 52, 249), 0, (topo, moho, -80.0); dz=0.5, spacing=:sbp).
  • Blocks. The mesh is split into blocks at grid rows where the material jumps along the full width. Their interfaces are grid lines, so the jump stays sharp. Other heterogeneity (a magma chamber, gradients) is sampled at the nodes. Give the nodes on an interface the material above it (z >= interface): where the node spacing does not change at the interface (e.g. a flat mesh), the interface is taken at the upper row of the jump, so the material below would shift it up by one row (a first-order error).
  • Oceans (fluids, vs = 0): a fluid layer is a block of its own, between two surfaces of the mesh (e.g. sea level and the bathymetry), and must span the whole width (no coastlines). At the seafloor the normal velocity and the normal traction are continuous and the tangential traction vanishes: the fluid slips along the solid (SAT terms on the normal components only), stable as all interfaces. Against the staggered grid with a flat seafloor (exact there) below 1 % at 100 m; with a sloping seafloor the staggered grid converges towards it. SH waves see the seafloor as a free surface. Strains at stations in the fluid: the volumetric strain, split equally between the normal components.
  • Time step and boundaries. The time step follows from the spectral radius of the operator: dt is not needed, give the duration tmax. The sides and the bottom absorb with impedance boundary conditions; absorber = :pml adds a perfectly matched layer of na = 20 nodes (at least 6; the CFS-PML of OpenSWPC on the ξ and η parts of the SBP operators: about 20 times fewer reflections, ~1.5× the cost per step with layers on all sides), :mpml the multiaxial PML (as on the staggered grid, mpml_ratio = 0.1: stable under soft surface layers, where the PML can grow exponentially; 2–3 times the reflections of the PML), :cerjan a sponge.
  • Stations with surface=true record exactly on the topography. Attenuation, moment tensors and forces work as on the staggered grid.
  • 3D: curvilinear_mesh(x, y, surfaces; dz, bottom) with surfaces z(x, y) (functions or surface CartData); columns are vertical in 3D too.
  • MPI and GPUs as on the staggered grid: nproc processes along x (2D) or (npx, npy) (3D); the columns are not split. Results are identical to a run on one process.
  • Gradients and Born modelling: misfit_gradient (2D and 3D, attenuation, MPI, GPUs) uses the exact discrete adjoint (checkpoint_steps as on the staggered grid); the gradient is per model node. Source gradients (location, moment tensor, force, timing, source time function) as on the staggered grid. born works as on the staggered grid (also with attenuation, on GPUs and with MPI).
  • Anisotropy (VTI) as on the staggered grid (fields epsilon, delta, gamma; the symmetry axis stays vertical on the deformed mesh), also in gradients and Born modelling.
  • Snapshots: ParaView files and callbacks of the whole wavefield, and netCDF planes (Snapshots: the free surface fs on the topography, xy planes and sections resampled at regular heights; :v, :u, :ps, also in pieces).
  • Checkpoints (checkpoint, checkpoint_every, restart) as on the staggered grid.
  • SH waves in 2D (wave = :sh), with everything above (attenuation, VTI with γ, PML, gradients, Born, MPI, GPUs).
  • Teleseismic waves (TeleseismicWave) as on the staggered grid: total-field / scattered-field injection along a box (the box reaches up to the topography; the model must be horizontally layered and elastic along its sides and bottom, where the topography must be flat), with the exact gradient. PlaneWave (OpenSWPC's initial plane wave) is not available on curvilinear grids: use TeleseismicWave.

examples/2D_curvilinear.jl: the model on its deformed mesh and the vertical velocity at stations on the topography.

  • Cost. A step costs about 1.3–2.5× one on the staggered grid with as many cells (2D and 3D, CPU and GPU). Near topography, however, the curvilinear grid reaches the same accuracy with about 8× coarser spacing (in 2D, 64× fewer cells, 8× fewer steps).

Anisotropy (VTI)

With any of the fields epsilon, delta, gamma (Thomsen parameters; missing ones are 0) the model is transversely isotropic with a vertical symmetry axis (VTI: layered sediments, horizontally aligned cracks or melt lenses). vp and vs are then the vertical velocities:

qPvp vertically, vp √(1 + 2ε) horizontally; δ shapes the wavefront near the vertical
SHvs vertically, vs √(1 + 2γ) horizontally
qSVvs vertically and horizontally, its anisotropy follows from ε − δ
model = CartData(X, Y, Z, (; rho, vp, vs, epsilon=fill(0.1, size(X)), delta=fill(0.05, size(X)), gamma=fill(0.08, size(X))))
  • The symmetry axis is the z axis of the grid: every stress needs only the strain rates at its own point, as in isotropic media, so the accuracy and the stability (time step: the fastest qP direction) are those of the isotropic code. Tilted axes (TTI) are not supported.
  • Attenuation: the normal stiffness is split as in the isotropic scheme into a P part (Qp) and the rest (Qs), and stays symmetric: qP waves along the axes attenuate with Qp, S waves with Qs; isotropic media give exactly the isotropic scheme.
  • Everything else works as for isotropic models: free surface and topography, oceans, PML, sources and stations between grid points, GPUs, MPI (also a DistributedModel created with anisotropy = true), gradients (misfit_gradient also returns epsilon, delta, gamma; exact, as checked against automatic differentiation) and Born modelling. Not yet: plane waves and teleseismic waves (their incoming field assumes isotropic layers).
  • Models without these fields run the isotropic kernels: anisotropy costs nothing when it is not used.

Finer cells near the surface

The spacing in z may vary: give the CartData the depths you want, for example fine cells in slow near-surface layers (sediments, volcanic deposits) and coarser ones at depth. The solver then works on a grid stretched in depth (the depth derivatives are taken along the cell index and divided by the local spacing, with 4th-order consistent factors), so that the waves see the same physics with far fewer cells. The spacing must change smoothly, by at most 25 % from cell to cell (a few percent is best). The spacing in x and y stays constant.

depths, h = [0.0025], 0.005                         # cell centres [km]: 5 m cells down to 250 m,
while depths[end] < 3.0                              # then 3 % larger per cell up to 40 m
    hn = depths[end] < 0.25 ? 0.005 : min(1.03h, 0.04)
    push!(depths, depths[end] + (h + hn) / 2)
    h = hn
end
z = [reverse(-depths); 0.0025; 0.0075]              # z up, with 2 cells of air on top
X, Y, Z = xyz_grid(range(0, 4, step=0.005), 0, z)

Everything else works as on a uniform grid: sources and stations (also between grid points), the PML (its profile follows the actual depths), MPI, GPUs, and the exact adjoint gradients. The time step is limited by the smallest cells, as before: dt < 6/7 / (vp √(1/dx² + 1/dz²)) layer by layer, so fine cells in slow rock cost little extra. Plane waves need a uniform grid.

Example: a 250 m thick layer with vs = 0.4 km/s over faster rock (4 Hz Ricker, P-SV, stations 10 m deep), compared with a uniform 5 m grid:

Models built per MPI process

A CartData holds the whole model, and in a run on several MPI processes every process sets up the whole medium before it keeps the part of its subdomain. A model that does not fit into the memory of one process is given as a DistributedModel instead: the coordinates of the whole grid and a function that builds the model of a range of columns. Every process calls it once, for its own subdomain:

x, y, z = range(0, 100, 2001), range(0, 80, 1601), range(-30, 2, 641)     # cell centres [km], z up
model = DistributedModel(x, y, z) do ix, iy
    X, Y, Z = xyz_grid(x[ix], y[iy], z)                                   # this part of the grid: use any GMG tool on it
    air = Z .> 0
    (rho=ifelse.(air, 1.0, 2700.0), vp=ifelse.(air, 0.0, 5.0), vs=ifelse.(air, 0.0, 2.9), Qp=fill(200.0, size(X)), Qs=fill(100.0, size(X)))
end
result = simulate(model; nproc=(2, 2), dt, nt, sources, stations)

The function returns a NamedTuple of arrays of size (length(ix), length(iy), length(z)) or a CartData, with the fields of a CartData model (with DistributedModel(...; anisotropy = true) also epsilon, delta, gamma). The results are those of the same model given as a CartData. The memory a process needs for the setup then grows with its subdomain, not with the model. Moment tensor and force sources at their cells (interpolation = :cell), stations, snapshots, checkpoints, GPUs and stretched grids work as usual; plane and teleseismic waves, sources between grid points, misfit gradients and Born modelling need a CartData. The wavefield at the end is not collected (wavefield = true does).

A curvilinear model is built per process in the same way, column by column: DistributedModel(f, x, y, nz) with the SBP nodes x, y of the columns and nz nodes per column; f(ix, iy) returns the deformed grid of these columns with its material, for example from GMG with a fixed number of cells per layer (so that all pieces have the same rows):

xs, ys = sbp_nodes(-100, 100, 2001), sbp_nodes(-80, 80, 1601)
model = DistributedModel(xs, ys, 1 + 60 + 140) do ix, iy
    g = curvilinear_grid(xs[ix], ys[iy], (topo, moho, -60.0), (60, 140); spacing=:sbp)
    low = g.z.val .< moho.(g.x.val, g.y.val)
    addfield(g, (; rho=ifelse.(low, 3300.0, 2700.0), vp=ifelse.(low, 8.0, 6.0), vs=ifelse.(low, 4.5, 3.5)))
end
result = simulate(model; nproc=(4, 4), tmax, sources, stations, absorber=:pml)

Every process then holds the mesh and the material of its own columns only (and a few more around them).

Geographic coordinates

Models built from geographic data with a GMG ProjectionPoint keep their projection: sources and stations can be given in longitude and latitude with the same ProjectionPoint (Sources and stations).

Models from thermomechanical and petrological models

The temperature of a MagmaThermoKinematics.jl model, or any other temperature field, can be turned into density, seismic velocities and melt fraction with MAGEMin phase diagrams, including the reduction of the velocities by partial melt and a low Q in partially molten rock. See the Unzen examples in Examples.

Random media

xi = random_medium(x, y, z; spectrum=:vonkarman, a=0.5, epsilon=0.05, kappa=0.3)
model = perturb(model, xi)

makes fluctuations with a Gaussian, exponential or von Kármán spectrum (anisotropic with a=(ax, ay, az)) and applies them as OpenSWPC does (vp, vs × (1 + ξ), ρ × (1 + 0.8 ξ) in solid rock). read_random_medium(file) reads OpenSWPC's random media.

Layered models and OpenSWPC files

read_lhm, read_lgm and read_grd build models from layered descriptions (with OpenSWPC's options vcut, munk and earth_flattening), and read_netcdf reads OpenSWPC's netCDF velocity models; see Coming from OpenSWPC. flatten_earth(model) applies the Earth-flattening transformation to any model.
