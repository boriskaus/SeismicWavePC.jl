Validation

On Marmousi2 (5 m grid, 3 s, an explosion and 161 receivers 22 m below the sea surface, PML) the three codes give the same waveforms. OpenSWPC agrees to 10⁻³ (vertical) and 0.7 % (horizontal velocity) with the same amplitude. SeismicWaves.jl agrees to 6 % (vertical) and 2 % (horizontal) after one amplitude factor per component (it uses the opposite sign of moment tensors and z down; its free surface is half a cell lower and its receivers are snapped to grid points, which changes amplitudes close to a free surface):

Pressure and horizontal velocity at a few cells below the free surface, for waves travelling along it, are the hardest case: there JUDI.jl, with 4th-order differences up to its (flat, mirrored) free surface, is twice as accurate; SeismicWavePC.jl uses 2nd-order differences next to free surfaces, as OpenSWPC, which also handles topography.

The SeismicWavePC.jl seismograms barely change between 5 m and 1.25 m, whereas the difference of JUDI.jl to them halves with every refinement: JUDI.jl converges to the same solution, but more slowly on this model, as expected when the material is defined at the grid nodes (the sea floor lies between two nodes) rather than in the cells.

Point force under a free surface: comparison with SPECFEM2D

Vertical displacement, each trace normalized to its peak, against SPECFEM2D (spectral elements), the optimal operators OPT3 and OPT5 of flexOPT.jl, and two 3-point finite-difference schemes (FD3, convFD3):

SeismicWavePC.jl (dotted) follows SPECFEM2D (black) in the direct P and S waves and in the waves reflected at the free surface. The other codes smooth the force with a Gaussian (σ = 500 m), SeismicWavePC.jl uses a point force; its traces are aligned in time by cross-correlation (the delay of its Ricker wavelet differs).

Free surface on topography

The same benchmark with topography (the dev/cutCells cases of flexOPT.jl, also by Nobuaki Fuji): the free surface tilted by 10, 20 and 30° (source, force along the normal and receivers rotated with it), and a Gaussian hill (3 km high, σ = 5 km) between the source and the receivers. Two lines of five receivers, 10 km ("deep") and 1 km ("shallow") below the surface; components along (ut) and normal to the surface (un). On the 500 m grid (16 points per S wavelength) the staggered grid represents the surface as a staircase of cells, with the default free surface or free_surface = :corners; the curvilinear solver (grid = :curvilinear, see Models) puts its nodes exactly on the surface (for the tilts on a mesh whose bottom is parallel to the surface, 80 km below it). The references: for the tilted planes the exact answer, the flat case rotated (on a 62.5 m grid, where its surface lies on cell faces); for the hill free_surface = :corners on a 62.5 m grid (examples/topography_benchmark.jl, under a minute on a laptop):

Away from the surface the staircase costs little (2–4.5 %). Within a fifth of an S wavelength of a sloping surface the errors are 20–30 %, mostly delays of the waves along the surface (u_t in the figure); :corners reduces them by 1.5–2. The curvilinear solver stays at about 1 % on both lines, as on a flat surface (the hill: at the accuracy of its reference, whose variants differ by up to 1 %).

On the same grid a curvilinear run costs about twice a staggered one (as many time steps here, but more work per node: 7th-order collocated stencils instead of 4th-order staggered ones), but it reaches the accuracy that the staggered grid needs 4× finer cells for (:corners at 125 m), about 10× faster. On a laptop GPU (Apple Metal, Float32, the same errors) these small 2D models run about as fast as on the CPU (0.06–0.4 s at 500 m, 0.65–1.5 s at 125 m; a time step costs at least ~0.15 ms there, mostly kernel launches), and the curvilinear solver at 500 m is about 3× faster than :corners at 125 m. GPUs pay off most for large models, such as the 10 m Unzen model of the README (8.5 million nodes). Near a sloping surface on the staggered grid, use a finer grid than the 16 points per S wavelength that suffice elsewhere, or the curvilinear solver.

Garvin's problem: exact solution on tilted surfaces

The test of de la Puente et al. (2014): Garvin's problem (Garvin, 1956), a line explosion 25 m below the free surface of a half-space (vp = 2 km/s, vs = 1 km/s, ρ = 1600 kg/m³, Ricker wavelet with its peak at 10 Hz, < 5 % above 23 Hz), five receivers on the surface 100–500 m from the source, the surface tilted by 0–40°. The reference is exact: garvin evaluates the Cagniard–de Hoop solution. The errors are the time-frequency envelope (EM) and phase (PM) misfits of Kristeková et al. (2006) (tf_misfits, 2–30 Hz) of the components along (vt) and normal to (vn) the surface, for the curvilinear solver on a mesh whose bottom is parallel to the surface (examples/garvin_benchmark.jl, under a minute on a laptop):

On tilted surfaces the curvilinear solver stays below 1 % already at 6 points per wavelength (de la Puente et al. report EM 1.3–4.5 % and PM 0.3–1 % for their mimetic scheme at that resolution), and the errors drop by an order of magnitude with each refinement step. On the flat surface the 6.67 m grid is the exception: the source, less than four nodes below the surface, excites a spurious surface mode at about 7 grid points per wavelength (here 21 Hz; 28 Hz on the 5 m grid), which hardly propagates and rings at the source and the nearest receivers (upper left). It is a property of the discrete free surface (other upwind SBP closures give it too) and is damped on sloping surfaces. Keep the source spectrum below it (8 points per minimum S wavelength: < 1 %), or the source a few wavelengths deep (25 m → 100 m: 9 % → 2 %).

Rayleigh waves in firn

Firn (compacting snow over glacial ice) has a strong velocity gradient: vs grows from about 300 m/s at the surface to 2000 m/s at 100 m depth, and Rayleigh waves above 5 Hz come in several modes with very different, depth-sensitive particle motions (Chaput et al., 2022, J. Glaciol. 68, 785–798). Their numerical results (Figs. 8 and 9) are Rayleigh modes of 1D profiles; here SeismicWavePC.jl is compared with a 1D eigen-solver of the same kind (linear finite elements as RAYLEE, test/rayleigh_modes.jl) for three profiles: smooth (as their base model), with random fine layering (their Fig. 8b) and with that layering 2 m deeper (their Fig. 9). A vertical force (Ricker, 20 Hz) just below the surface, 501 surface stations at 100–1100 m, 0.5 m grid; the slant stack of the stations at each frequency gives the phase velocity, H/V and sense of the particle motion of each excited mode (examples/firn_rayleigh_benchmark.jl):

The modes R0–R3 follow the eigen-solver: the phase velocity to 0.15–0.5 % on average (2.4 % at 6 Hz, where the 1 km line spans only four wavelengths), H/V to 1–6 % on average (more right at the cutoff frequency of a mode, where it is hardly excited), and the sense of the motion is right at all but one of 329 picks. As in the paper, the fundamental mode R0 is retrograde, R1 prograde with H/V ≈ 1, and R2 strongly horizontal (H/V 3–8); the 2 m shift of the layering moves the features of R1 and R2.

The motion of these modes changes within centimetres of the surface. Surface stations record at the centre of the top cell (0.25 m deep here), and the eigen-solver is evaluated there too: at the surface itself the H/V of the fundamental mode at 45 Hz is 0.85 instead of 0.68. Field instruments in firn are usually buried by about this much.

The test suite checks the fundamental mode over a smooth gradient (vs from 800 to 2000 m/s, 10 m grid) against the eigen-solver: phase velocity to 0.5 % and H/V to 3 % at 16–27 points per S wavelength at the surface. The error grows with frequency roughly as f², as expected from the 2nd-order differences next to the free surface: 0.2 % at 20 points per S wavelength, 1 % at 10.

Teleseismic plane waves

TeleseismicWave (a plane wave from below, injected along a box) against the analytical solution of a homogeneous half-space (vp = 6 km/s, vs = 3.46 km/s, 100 m grid, Ricker wavelet of 1 Hz): the incoming P (20°) or SV (15°) wave and the P and SV waves reflected at the free surface, whose amplitudes follow from the traction-free surface. Displacement at the surface and at 3–28 km depth (examples/teleseismic_benchmark.jl):

The relative L2 differences are 0.1–0.2 % (0.5 % for SV at the surface). Also checked:

  • a crust (30 km, vp = 6 km/s) over a mantle, P, SV and SH, against the exact layered solution: 0.1–0.25 %; the same on a grid stretched in depth (50 m at the surface, 200 m at depth): 0.1–0.6 %;
  • 3D, a P wave from the backazimuth 240° and an SH wave from 200° (oblique to the grid) in a crust over a mantle: all three components to 0.06–0.2 %;
  • the free-surface response of a half-space, P at 0–40°: the ratio of horizontal and vertical motion exact to 2·10⁻⁴, the same amplitude everywhere along the surface (10⁻⁴), waves leaking out of the box 4–8·10⁻⁴ (PlaneWave, the OpenSWPC initial wavefield, gives 4 % of spurious horizontal motion at vertical incidence and is cut at oblique incidence);
  • misfit gradients with a teleseismic wave against Enzyme (round-off, also at the box boundary), finite differences, and MPI.

The tests also check:

  • misfit gradients against automatic differentiation (Enzyme, agree to round-off) and finite differences of whole simulations, in 2D and 3D, with attenuation and waves in the PML; source gradients, misfit options and several shots against finite differences;
  • Rayleigh waves over a velocity gradient against the eigen-solver (see Rayleigh waves in firn);
  • MPI runs with 4 processes against serial runs (identical; also the gradients);
  • CPU against Metal (agree to 10⁻⁶) and against CUDA on an NVIDIA A100 (examples/check_cuda.jl: forward simulations and gradients agree to 10⁻¹⁴ in double precision), and MPI on two A100 GPUs against a serial run (examples/check_cuda_mpi.jl, also 10⁻¹⁴);
  • the interfaces to Optim, LogDensityProblems, ChainRules and ForwardDiff;
  • type stability with JET.jl.
